\begin{abstract}
Latent world models predict future states from visual observations and action sequences within a learned representation space. When environment dynamics vary, accurate prediction requires inferring hidden dynamic properties from interaction history. However, because models can exploit immediate visual shortcuts or regress to average dynamics to achieve low loss without history conditioning, conventional prediction accuracy fails to reveal genuine adaptation. We introduce ContextWorld, a benchmark designed to evaluate in-context dynamics adaptation in action-conditioned latent world models under strictly frozen weights. ContextWorld employs paired counterfactual evaluations—varying interaction histories while holding the current observation and future action sequence fixed—to concurrently quantify adaptation accuracy (whether predictions correctly distinguish between diverging future states) and history sensitivity (whether predictions respond to altered context and yield lower error than mismatched history). Spanning nine tasks across four environments, the benchmark provides separate training and evaluation splits with continuous interaction rollouts under differing dynamics. Our systematic evaluation of representative latent world models (LeWM, PLDM, and DINO-WM) yields three key findings: First, models trained under fixed dynamics make negligible use of interaction history across most tasks. Second, mixing multi-dynamics data improves history responsiveness, but the benefits of data scaling and the relative effectiveness of training strategies vary substantially across tasks. Third, under certain training schemes, models fail to effectively leverage history even on the training distribution, despite steadily decreasing training loss. These results reveal a notable mismatch between prediction-loss optimization and in-context dynamics adaptation, suggesting that standard prediction objectives do not reliably drive models to use interaction history and highlighting the need for objectives and benchmarks explicitly targeted at in-context dynamics adaptation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Visual world models learn environment dynamics to predict future states~\cite{ha2018worldmodels}, with latent world models performing these predictions within learned representations of visual observations~\cite{hafner2019planet,hafner2020dreamer}.
A model trained under fixed dynamics can achieve high prediction accuracy without utilizing interaction history to adapt its predictions to current conditions.
However, such predictions can fail when environment dynamics vary~\cite{wang2026context}.
For example, visually identical objects can move different distances under the same push if surface friction differs.
Interaction history provides evidence about these hidden physical properties.
We investigate whether latent world models can leverage this contextual evidence to predict future states under varied dynamics \emph{without updating their weights}---an ability we term \emph{in-context dynamics adaptation}.

Physical prediction benchmarks evaluate whether observed interactions help models infer hidden properties or predict downstream outcomes governed by them~\cite{baradel2020cophy,tung2023physionpp}.
While benchmarks like Physion++ assess physical understanding via auxiliary readout classifiers, we introduce ContextWorld to evaluate in-context dynamics adaptation directly within action-conditioned latent world models, leveraging their existing latent predictors without training separate classifiers for future events.
The benchmark spans nine tasks across four environments, covering physical parameter variations and interaction rules, and provides separate training and evaluation data.

In its paired tasks, two dynamics conditions share the identical current observation and future action sequence, but produce diverging ground-truth futures.
Interaction history is therefore strictly necessary to distinguish the two outcomes.
Because a deterministic model that ignores history receives identical inputs and yields identical predictions for both conditions, it scores at most 50\% on paired future selection when ties count as failures.
This paired counterfactual design restricts history-agnostic heuristics, enabling us to concurrently evaluate two aspects directly in latent space: (1) adaptation accuracy, which measures whether a model's prediction correctly aligns with its corresponding true future rather than the alternative condition's future; and (2) history sensitivity, which measures whether predictions reliably respond to altered context, verified by substituting the history with that from an alternate dynamics setting to examine if matching history yields strictly lower error.

Using ContextWorld, we benchmark three representative latent world models: LeWM~\cite{maes2026leworldmodel}, PLDM~\cite{sobal2025pldm}, and DINO-WM~\cite{zhou2025dinowm}.
We find that after training under fixed dynamics, these models make negligible use of interaction history across most tasks.
To examine how this adaptation ability can be acquired, we mix ContextWorld training data with original rollouts and compare training from scratch against fine-tuning models pretrained under fixed dynamics.
We further scale up the training data and examine the impact of freezing the visual encoder.
Our experiments reveal that while data scaling improves history utilization on several tasks, the optimal model and training strategy vary substantially by task.
For example, starting from the same pretrained weights, freezing PLDM's visual encoder improves its ability to distinguish future states under varying contact friction, but reduces this ability under motion damping.

To understand why models often fail to leverage history, we analyze model predictions on the training distribution itself.
Under certain training schemes, training loss steadily decreases or approaches convergence even while models fail to use interaction history to distinguish physical conditions.
Because this failure occurs on the training data, it cannot be explained by out-of-distribution generalization alone, suggesting that standard prediction objectives may not inherently provide sufficient incentive for models to condition their predictions on interaction history.

Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The ContextWorld benchmark and paired evaluation protocol.} We introduce a standardized framework featuring separate training and evaluation data across nine hidden-dynamics tasks. This paired design quantifies both adaptation accuracy and history sensitivity directly from action-conditioned latent rollouts.
\item \textbf{Empirical findings on adaptation limits.} We benchmark representative models across data scales, training strategies, and visual encoder freezing schemes, demonstrating that optimal strategies are highly task-dependent. Furthermore, we show that training loss can steadily decrease even when models fail to leverage history on the training data, revealing a disconnect between standard prediction losses and in-context dynamics adaptation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Visual world models.}
Visual world models learn environment dynamics to simulate and predict future states.
Early frameworks~\cite{ha2018worldmodels} combined learned visual representations with recurrent dynamics to support internal policy training.
Subsequent methods advanced this paradigm through online planning (PlaNet~\cite{hafner2019planet}) and policy learning via imagined latent trajectories (Dreamer~\cite{hafner2020dreamer}).
While these early approaches rely on pixel-level image reconstruction to learn representations, Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs)~\cite{lecun2022path} shift the training objective toward predicting target representations directly without reconstructing pixels.
This principle has been applied to masked spatial regions (I-JEPA~\cite{assran2023ijepa}) and extended to temporal video representation learning (V-JEPA~\cite{bardes2024vjepa}).
To support robotic planning, V-JEPA~2~\cite{assran2025vjepa2} introduces an action-conditioned predictor trained on robot interactions atop pretrained video representations.

Action-conditioned latent world models also differ in how they obtain their visual representations.
DINO-WM~\cite{zhou2025dinowm} trains a predictive dynamics model on frozen pretrained features for planning from offline trajectories.
In contrast, PLDM~\cite{sobal2025pldm} jointly optimizes the visual encoder and the predictor using reward-free offline data.
To stabilize joint training, LeWM~\cite{maes2026leworldmodel} simplifies the objective to a latent prediction loss and a single representation regularizer.
We benchmark these three paradigms to evaluate how structural choices influence in-context dynamics adaptation.

\paragraph{In-context adaptation.}
When environment dynamics differ between training and deployment, interaction history provides a signal for adaptation without requiring weight updates.
Meta-reinforcement learning~\cite{beck2025metarl} leveraged this principle by training agents to adapt from limited experience across tasks.
RL$^2$~\cite{duan2016rl2} implicitly stores interaction history within a recurrent network's hidden state, while PEARL~\cite{rakelly2019pearl} infers a task representation from experience to condition the policy.
Paralleling large language models, which learn from in-context examples without parameter updates~\cite{brown2020fewshot,dong2024icl}, this adaptation paradigm has been extended to visual world models.

Within world models, context guides predictions of action outcomes in new environments.
OSWM~\cite{ferreira2024oswm} pretrains a transformer on synthetic dynamics, using observed state transitions as context to forecast futures.
L2World~\cite{wang2026context} distinguishes using context to identify previously seen environments from learning new dynamics.
It studies the effects of context length and training environment diversity through multi-step prediction errors and context perturbations.
For robotic control, ICWM~\cite{wang2026icwm} employs task-agnostic interaction history to adapt policies to novel camera viewpoints without weight updates, using ablations over correct, absent, and mismatched contexts to validate history reliance.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
To evaluate adaptation across environments, existing benchmarks introduce controlled variations in dynamics, appearance, or task rules.
Meta-World~\cite{yu2020metaworld} and Procgen~\cite{cobbe2020procgen} test generalization across manipulation tasks and procedurally generated levels.
CausalWorld~\cite{ahmed2021causalworld} and CARL~\cite{benjamins2023context} vary environment properties, while the XLand series (XLand-MiniGrid~\cite{nikulin2024xland}, XLand-100B~\cite{nikulin2025xland100b}) evaluates policy adaptation from interaction history across distributions of tasks.

For physical prediction, several benchmarks focus on counterfactual reasoning and hidden property inference.
CoPhy~\cite{baradel2020cophy} asks models to predict outcomes after changes to a scene's initial configuration, using the original trajectory as evidence of hidden properties.
Filtered-CoPhy~\cite{janny2022filteredcophy} ensures the observed trajectory contains sufficient information for the counterfactual prediction.
ComPhy~\cite{chen2022comphy} probes reasoning regarding hidden mass and charge through predictive and counterfactual video querying.
Physion++~\cite{tung2023physionpp} tests whether interaction history helps predict future contact in paired trials that share the same current observation but differ in physical properties, assessing this understanding via auxiliary readout classifiers.

Beyond visual domains, a recent time-series benchmark~\cite{zhang2026mechanism} reveals that low prediction error can coexist with incorrect response directions when actions change.
This echoes our finding of a mismatch between prediction-loss minimization and true dynamics adaptation.
A remaining question is when action-conditioned latent world models learn to leverage history for accurate prediction.
ContextWorld addresses this gap through controlled comparisons across models, tasks, data scales, and training strategies.


\section{ContextWorld Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

ContextWorld is designed to evaluate in-context dynamics adaptation---specifically, whether models use interaction history to accurately predict future states conditioned on an action sequence.
This section formalizes the prediction setting, describes how we construct the tasks and datasets, and explains the paired evaluation protocol and metrics.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

To isolate in-context adaptation from weight updates, model parameters $\theta$ remain fixed during evaluation.
The model receives exclusively visual observations and actions.
For a target horizon $\tau$, the model receives an interaction history $H_t$, comprising interleaved visual observations up to time $t$ and their executed actions, alongside a future action sequence $A_{t:t+\tau-1}$.
The prediction and its target are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat z_{t+\tau} &= f_\theta(H_t,A_{t:t+\tau-1}),\\
z_{t+\tau} &= e_\theta(o_{t+\tau}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:prediction-setting}
\end{equation}
Here, $o_{t+\tau}$ is the ground-truth observation at time $t+\tau$, and $e_\theta$ is the model's target encoder, which is also frozen.
Predictions are evaluated directly against these encoded targets in latent space.


\subsection{Task Design}
\label{sec:tasks}

To evaluate dynamics adaptation, we construct nine tasks spanning four base environments by introducing controlled variations in physical parameters and interaction rules.
These environments build upon TwoRoom navigation~\cite{zhou2025dinowm}, the PushT pushing environment~\cite{chi2023diffusionpolicy}, Reacher from the DeepMind Control Suite~\cite{tassa2018control}, and Cube from OGBench~\cite{park2025ogbench}.
The tasks and their history requirements are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:tasks}.
Importantly, models are trained separately for each task, and baselines like DINO-WM keep their pretrained visual encoder frozen throughout.

To isolate the role of interaction history, we construct paired counterfactual samples for six tasks: pusher displacement, arm mass, contact friction, motion damping, gripper carry rule, and portal exit.
Within each pair, the two samples share the same current observation and future action sequence, but operate under different hidden dynamics.
The history and ground-truth future for each sample are generated through one continuous simulation with fixed dynamics.
Because the resulting futures differ physically, accurate prediction necessitates conditioning on the interaction history.
Trajectory pairing and validity checks are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:trajectory-pairing}.

Unlike the paired counterfactuals, the remaining three tasks evaluate history usage across multiple condition values or specific environmental rules.
The speed task tests generalization to unseen velocities: we hold the current observation and planned actions fixed, providing histories generated at varying speeds to observe if the model scales its predicted motion.
Action delay introduces variable latency to test predictions before and after an action takes effect.
For example, delays of six or ten simulator steps both result in zero movement within the next five steps.
Because these yield identical future states, they are scored as a single response group, with all groups weighted equally (Appendix~\ref{app:delay-groups}).
Finally, the door traversability task tests whether the model correctly infers passage or blockage from preceding interactions.

\subsection{Dataset Construction and Splits}
\label{sec:data}

To evaluate whether models infer dynamics from context rather than simply recalling training trajectories, all tasks provide trajectory-disjoint Training, Development, and Test splits.
The dataset design covers two facets of dynamics adaptation: (1) \textbf{in-context mode disambiguation}, where paired physical-parameter tasks such as friction, mass, and damping share parameter settings across splits but evaluate models on unseen counterfactual trajectories; and (2) \textbf{continuous parameter generalization}, exemplified by Speed, which evaluates interpolation and extrapolation to speeds held out during training.
Each paired task provides up to 32,768 training pairs, with 256 balanced pairs in each of Development and Test.
Development supports method and checkpoint selection; Test is reserved for final evaluation.
Comprehensive dataset statistics, dynamics settings, and split checks appear in Table~\ref{tab:dataset-sizes} and Appendix~\ref{app:dataset-splits}.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation}
Evaluating latent in-context dynamics adaptation requires disentangling genuine context utilization from predictive shortcuts.
In learned latent spaces, models can achieve low prediction loss by averaging over dynamics conditions or minimizing prediction errors shared across dynamics conditions, without adapting to the underlying physical properties.
To rigorously evaluate models under strictly frozen weights, we establish an evaluation protocol structured around four complementary questions: (1) Does the prediction distinguish diverging physical futures? (2) How accurate is the multi-step trajectory rollout? (3) Does the supplied history reliably reduce prediction error? and (4) Is the differential response to varying dynamics accurately recovered?

\paragraph{Adaptation Accuracy ($A_{\mathrm{future}}$).}
To test whether predictions distinguish diverging physical outcomes, we evaluate paired counterfactual predictions against latent ground-truth targets.
For pair $i$ in a set of $N$ pairs, with latent targets $z_i^A, z_i^B$ and predictions $\hat{z}_i^A, \hat{z}_i^B$, a prediction is scored as correct only if it is strictly closer in MSE ($d$) to its corresponding future than to the alternative: $I_i^A = \mathbf{1}[d(\hat{z}_i^A, z_i^A) < d(\hat{z}_i^A, z_i^B)]$, with $I_i^B$ defined symmetrically.
The paired adaptation accuracy is:
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{future}} = \frac{100}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( I_i^A + I_i^B \right).
\label{eq:future-score}
\end{equation}
Ties count as failures; thus, any deterministic model that ignores history receives identical inputs and scores at most 50\%.
For multi-condition tasks, Speed requires the matching history to yield lower error than all alternative speed contexts, while Action Delay evaluates classification across six latency response groups (Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-details}).

\paragraph{Multi-Step Prediction Error ($E/B$) and History Controls.}
Because high classification accuracy does not guarantee low trajectory error---as even minute perturbations toward the correct target can yield 100\% accuracy (Appendix~\ref{app:scoring-controls})---we measure complete autoregressive rollouts across up to 25 simulator steps without intermediate ground-truth observations.
We normalize the aggregate rollout error across scenes by a reference that does not distinguish dynamics conditions~\cite{murphy1988skill}:
\begin{equation}
E/B = \frac{\sum_{q} L_q}{\sum_{q} B_q}.
\label{eq:complete-error}
\end{equation}
For scene $q$, $L_q$ denotes the mean squared prediction error averaged over $K_q$ dynamics conditions, $C_q$ shared action sequences, and $T=5$ rollout steps, while $B_q$ is the corresponding error of the mean-future reference $\bar{z}_{qct} = K_q^{-1} \sum_k z_{qkct}$.
For equally weighted conditions sharing the current observation and future actions, this mean future minimizes squared error among predictions that do not distinguish the conditions~\cite{gneiting2011point}; consequently, any deterministic model that ignores history cannot achieve $E/B < 1$.
Lower $E/B$ is better, with $E/B = 0$ denoting exact prediction.

To isolate the effect of the supplied history on prediction error, we swap histories across dynamics conditions while keeping the current observation, future actions, and target fixed.
We report \textbf{History (\%)} (the win-rate where matched history yields strictly lower error than mismatched history) and the normalized error reduction $G_H = (E_- - E_+) / B$, where $E_-$ and $E_+$ are aggregate errors under mismatched and matched histories, respectively, and $B = \sum_q B_q$.

\paragraph{Normalized Response Error (NRE).}
Prediction loss can decrease as errors shared across conditions shrink, even when the predicted difference between conditions remains inaccurate.
To isolate the model's conditional response to physical differences, we define the Normalized Response Error (NRE) over true difference vectors $\Delta z_i = z_i^B - z_i^A$ and predicted differences $\Delta \hat{z}_i = \hat{z}_i^B - \hat{z}_i^A$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NRE} = \frac{\sum_{i} \|\Delta \hat{z}_i - \Delta z_i\|^2}{\sum_{i} \|\Delta z_i\|^2}.
\label{eq:response-error}
\end{equation}
$\mathrm{NRE} = 0$ denotes an exact differential response, whereas $\mathrm{NRE} = 1$ is the baseline of predicting zero difference.
Subtracting predictions removes condition-invariant errors, measuring how accurately the model predicts the difference between dynamics conditions.

\paragraph{Evaluation Boundaries.}
Both $E/B$ and NRE are evaluated in each checkpoint's target representation, not a shared physical space.
Changes in that representation must also be considered when comparing training schemes or checkpoints.
We report means and standard deviations across runs, with multi-step trajectory intervals estimated via source-cluster bootstrap resampling (Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-details}).
Retention of control capabilities in the original environments is evaluated separately via CEM planning (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\subsection{Adaptation Results}
\label{sec:adaptation-results}
Models trained on fixed-dynamics data show limited ability to distinguish futures under different dynamics: Original scores near or below 50\% on the seven binary tasks (Table~\ref{tab:test-results}). Adding ContextWorld data improves several tasks, including arm mass for LeWM and PLDM, but does not produce uniformly strong adaptation.

Model advantages also depend on the task. With Mixed training, LeWM and PLDM achieve near-perfect action-delay accuracy, whereas DINO-WM scores 16.65\%. On pusher displacement, DINO-WM leads at 97.66\%, while LeWM and PLDM remain much closer to the 50\% reference. All three reach near-perfect door-traversability accuracy. These rank reversals motivate evaluation across diverse dynamics rather than a single representative task.

Strong one-step adaptation does not establish accurate multi-step prediction. On pusher displacement, LeWM and PLDM trained with Original $\to$ Mixed (Frozen Encoder) exceed 98\% History win-rate and substantially reduce NRE (Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}). Yet Figure~\ref{fig:multistep-prediction} shows that their prediction errors on extended action sequences exceed the corresponding mean-future reference at later horizons, despite improving on Original. Aggregate $E/B$ remains above 1.4 for both models. These queries extend both the actions and prediction horizon relative to the one-step tasks; the contrast does not isolate a single cause of rollout error.

\subsection{Effects of Training Choices}
\label{sec:training-effects}
Freezing the pretrained visual encoder helps some tasks and hurts others. For PLDM, it improves contact friction Test accuracy from 50.00\% to 73.63\% relative to joint training, but reduces motion damping accuracy from 79.88\% to 54.49\% (Table~\ref{tab:test-results}). Each comparison holds the data, initialization, and training duration fixed. Because projectors and action encoders remain trainable, this intervention does not isolate a static target representation.

PLDM on damping loses 8.01 percentage points of Development accuracy and 10.67 points of original-environment CEM success; CEM success decreases in all four comparisons (Appendix~\ref{app:model-capacity}). The cause of this regression remains unresolved.\par

\subsection{Prediction Loss and Adaptation}
\label{sec:loss-adaptation}
Weak evaluation performance may reflect poor generalization or conditional responses that were not learned well during training. We compare prediction loss with NRE on fixed training queries at epochs 5 and 10 (Figure~\ref{fig:training-evidence}).

On friction, PLDM joint training reduces prediction loss by 18.05\%, yet training-query NRE remains near the zero-response reference. On damping with the encoder frozen, loss approaches convergence while NRE improves but remains high (Figure~\ref{fig:training-evidence-full}). Changing the scheme helps: freezing yields a stronger conditional response on friction, whereas joint training performs better on damping, with Development showing the same task-dependent ordering.

These results expose a disconnect between prediction-loss optimization and history-conditioned adaptation, even on training data. Prediction loss aggregates errors across mixed data, whereas NRE directly measures error in the predicted differences between conditions. Optimizing the former does not ensure an accurate conditional response. This finding identifies insufficient constraints on history-conditioned dynamics in the evaluated training objectives and strategies.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
ContextWorld establishes a standardized benchmark to evaluate in-context dynamics adaptation in action-conditioned latent visual world models under strictly frozen weights. Across nine tasks spanning four simulated environments, our paired history comparisons decouple whether models condition their predictions on interaction history from how accurately they forecast the resulting futures. Systematic evaluations of LeWM, PLDM, and DINO-WM demonstrate that while training on multi-dynamics data can improve adaptation, relative model advantages and the impact of visual encoder freezing remain task-dependent. Crucially, our training-distribution diagnostics expose a disconnect between prediction-loss optimization and in-context adaptation: conditional responses can remain poorly captured even as training loss steadily decreases or approaches convergence. These findings identify insufficient constraints on history-conditioned dynamics within the evaluated training objectives and strategies, underscoring the need for direct adaptation benchmarks and formulations that explicitly constrain history-conditioned responses.

Our findings are framed by specific diagnostic boundaries. Predictions are evaluated in each model's native representation rather than a shared physical error space; most tasks examine adaptation among dynamics conditions represented in training, while Speed evaluates unseen parameter values; and mixed-data checkpoints are trained independently per task, with planning experiments quantifying capability retention in unperturbed environments. These boundaries outline clear avenues for future research: establishing complementary cross-model evaluation in grounded physical coordinates, scaling toward generalist multi-task world models that generalize across broader dynamics, and examining closed-loop planning to test whether history-conditioned latent predictions translate directly into improved decision-making.

\section{Trajectory Construction and Pairing}
\label{app:trajectory-pairing}

\paragraph{Continuous trajectories.}
Each sample follows a continuous simulator trajectory from its history through its future, without intermediate resets or state overrides.
Identical current observations may still conceal differences in velocity or contact state, so we perform task-specific checks of physical-state matching at the start of prediction.
For action delay, we retain the actual queue of pending actions.

\paragraph{Starting positions.}
For tasks in which the two dynamics conditions cannot naturally share a starting position, we exchange their starting positions during generation and verify that the two conditions have identical sets of starting images.
This check reduces the possibility that the starting image alone reveals the condition.

\paragraph{Predictions without history.}
Consider a complete, balanced pair with distinguishable encoded targets.
A deterministic model that ignores history receives the same current observation and future action sequence for both samples and therefore produces the same prediction.
This prediction cannot be strictly closer to each sample's own target than to the other target.
Consequently, such a model scores at most 50\% on the paired future-selection metric in Eq.~\ref{eq:future-score}; ties count as failures.

\section{Evaluation Details}
\label{app:evaluation-details}

\paragraph{Paired tasks.}
The strict comparisons in Eq.~\ref{eq:future-score} do not count ties as correct.
The two comparisons within a pair are dependent.
Appendix~\ref{app:trajectory-pairing} explains the upper bound for a deterministic model that ignores history.

\paragraph{Speed.}
For each target speed, accuracy is the proportion of cases in which matching history gives strictly lower prediction error than every alternative history.
We then average accuracy equally across target speeds.
Ties do not count as correct: if all histories produce the same prediction, accuracy is zero.
The main results use the interpolation speeds listed in Appendix~\ref{app:dataset-splits}.

\paragraph{Action delay.}
We select the candidate true future with the lowest mean squared error, resolving ties in favor of the smaller delay.
The prediction is correct when the selected delay and the true delay belong to the same response group.
Within each case, we first average correctness over delays in each group, then average equally across the six groups defined in Appendix~\ref{app:delay-groups}.
The final accuracy is the mean over cases.

\paragraph{Door traversability.}
For a prediction made with matching history, we compare the mean squared errors to the passage and blockage futures.
The prediction is correct only if the error to the true future is strictly smaller than the error to the alternative future.
Accuracy is averaged over cases and the two rules; ties do not count as correct.

\paragraph{Multi-step prediction horizons.}
Each prediction step advances five simulator steps.
We measure prediction errors at 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 simulator steps after the current observation.

\paragraph{Within-scene aggregation.}
Let $z_{qkct}$ and $\hat z_{qkct}$ denote the true and predicted future representations for scene $q$, dynamics condition $k$, action sequence $c$, and prediction time $t$.
With $K_q$ conditions, $C_q$ shared action sequences, and $T=5$ prediction times, the errors used in Eq.~\ref{eq:complete-error} are
\[
\begin{aligned}
L_q&=\frac{1}{K_qC_qT}\sum_{k,c,t}\|\hat z_{qkct}-z_{qkct}\|^2,\\
B_q&=\frac{1}{K_qC_qT}\sum_{k,c,t}\|z_{qkct}-\bar z_{qct}\|^2,\\
\bar z_{qct}&=\frac{1}{K_q}\sum_k z_{qkct}.
\end{aligned}
\]
L2World tests history dependence by shuffling context observations~\cite{wang2026context}; our controls instead swap histories between dynamics conditions.
For history controls, $E_+$ and $E_-$ sum the matching- and mismatched-history scene errors using the same averaging over conditions, action sequences, and prediction times.
When a sample has multiple mismatched histories, their errors are averaged equally.
A positive $G_H$ indicates that matching history reduces error; it does not imply a low complete prediction error.

\paragraph{Mean-future reference.}
The reference in Eq.~\ref{eq:complete-error} is used only for scoring and is never provided to the model.
The ratio is defined only when $\sum_q B_q>0$.
When conditions share the current observation and future actions and are equally weighted, a deterministic model that ignores history cannot achieve $E/B<1$: it must make the same prediction across conditions, and the mean-future reference minimizes the error of such a prediction.

\paragraph{Response error settings.}
We specify the prediction horizon for each NRE analysis.
The ratio in Eq.~\ref{eq:response-error} is defined only when $\sum_i\|\Delta z_i\|^2>0$.
NRE equal to 1 does not by itself show that a model ignores history.
Errors shared by both predictions cancel in the difference, so an exact conditional response does not imply exact complete predictions.

\paragraph{Uncertainty estimates.}
\label{app:result-uncertainty}
For multi-step errors, we use cluster bootstrap resampling~\cite{efron1993bootstrap,field2007cluster} at the level of source trajectories or scenes, retaining related samples together.
These intervals describe scene-sampling uncertainty for the evaluated checkpoints, not variability across training runs.

\paragraph{Interpreting multiple metrics.}
The metrics describe different aspects of the same predictions and are not independent pieces of evidence.

\section{Training and Diagnostic Settings}
\label{app:training-settings}

\paragraph{Optimization.}
The training recipe specifies 10 epochs per stage on eight GPUs, with 128 windows per device and no gradient accumulation, giving a global batch size of 1,024.
LeWM and PLDM use AdamW with learning rate $5\times10^{-5}$ and weight decay 0.001.
DINO-WM uses learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$ and zero weight decay; its pretrained DINOv2-small visual encoder remains frozen.
The models retain their respective prediction and regularization losses.
The Original $\rightarrow$ Mixed schemes load model weights but reset optimizer state.
Encoder freezing fixes both parameters and training-state buffers; it does not freeze the projector or action encoder.

\paragraph{Input and sampling.}
The standard setup uses three observation frames, five simulator steps between frames, and a one-frame prediction target.
Action delay mixed training instead uses seven-frame history.
The original-environment data are the TwoRoom, PushT, Reacher, and Cube datasets released with LeWM; each task uses only the dataset for its environment.
Only the ContextWorld Training split is mixed into training.
For component lengths $N_{\mathrm{orig}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{CW}}$, the mixture has a nominal epoch length of $2\max(N_{\mathrm{orig}},N_{\mathrm{CW}})$ windows before partitioning and batching, with equal counts from the two components.
The smaller component is sampled repeatedly.
Consequently, equal epoch counts do not guarantee equal update counts across data scales, and the two-stage schemes include additional original-environment training.

\paragraph{Prediction diagnostics.}
The main task comparison uses Test.
The multi-step and history diagnostics below use Development, with the same checkpoint identities where the input protocol is comparable.
Multi-step evaluation covers 2,436 source scenes across the nine tasks and predicts at 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 simulator steps using only the initial observed history.
The candidate actions and multi-step targets extend the one-step task queries.
Figure~\ref{fig:multistep-prediction} reports $E_t/B_t$: prediction and mean-future errors are summed over queries separately at each horizon, normalized within each checkpoint, then averaged equally over training repetitions. The aggregate $E/B$ instead combines errors over all horizons before division; it is not the arithmetic mean of these per-horizon ratios. The pointwise intervals use the same source-cluster bootstrap protocol.
Each checkpoint is scored separately, and matching training repetitions are then averaged equally.
Intervals use 4,000 source-cluster bootstrap samples; all conditions, actions, and times from a source remain together.
They condition on the evaluated checkpoints and do not estimate variation from retraining the model.
Original action-delay checkpoints do not have the native seven-frame interface and are excluded from this comparison.
DINO-WM has no jointly updated visual-encoder scheme; its comparable Original $\rightarrow$ Mixed results cover Speed, damping, and gripper carry.

\paragraph{Planning evaluation.}
CEM results measure success in the original environments, with no hidden-dynamics history intervention.
Most checkpoint evaluations use six evaluation seeds with 50 episodes each; some original LeWM and PLDM references use three sets of 100 episodes or an aggregated 300-episode result.
Evaluation repetitions are pooled within each checkpoint, then training repetitions are averaged equally.
A single original-environment checkpoint supplies the reference for all tasks from that environment; these repeated entries are not independent experiments.

\paragraph{Training-query analysis.}
The friction and damping comparisons use 32,768 training pairs, identical original-environment initialization within each task, and the same 50/50 mixture, batch size, and duration.
We evaluate 512 fixed training pairs and 256 Development pairs per task at epochs 5 and 10, predicting the next observation five simulator steps ahead.
Loss summaries use the logged mixed-training batches, approximately 50--70 records per epoch, rather than every training batch.
Checkpoints and logs are aligned by update step.
The curves show recorded means and batch interquartile ranges; loss medians give the same monotonic direction for friction joint training from epochs 5 to 10.
NRE is evaluated in each checkpoint's target representation and is not a common physical error across checkpoints.
For damping with the encoder frozen, prediction loss changes by only $-0.39\%$ between these endpoints and fluctuates in between. The late-training plateau is consistent with the loss approaching convergence.
